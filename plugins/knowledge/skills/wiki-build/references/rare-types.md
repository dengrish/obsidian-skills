# Rare entity types — the ten uncommon `type:` values, and the date parenthetical

Scope: the ten uncommon `type:` values, the Person and Event date parenthetical, and typography for works, organisms and genes.

---

## The ten rare types

These are the `type:` enum values beyond the five common ones. The enum is closed — no other values.

- `Device` — discrete physical instruments, hardware platforms, and pieces of equipment treated as subjects in their own right. Examples: `PacBio Revio`, `Illumina NovaSeq`, `NVIDIA A100`, microscopes, spectrometers, sequencing machines, lab instruments. The body covers the device's capabilities, design choices, throughput/specs where relevant, and how it fits in the broader landscape — the same shape as a `Software` entry minus the API surface, since physical instruments don't have one in the software sense.
- `Event` — specific recurring or one-time events with a name and a date. Covers both **research events** — conferences (`NeurIPS`), workshops, competitions (`ILSVRC`, titled under `references/writing.md` §1's acronym rule), named experiments, scientific episodes (`AI winter`) — and **historical events of any kind** — wars (`World War II`), revolutions (`French Revolution`), pandemics (`COVID-19 pandemic`), discoveries (`Discovery of penicillin`), named episodes (`Apollo 11 mission`). The defining test: a specific named happening (or named period) that occurred at an identifiable point or span in time. Not for general phenomena or ongoing trends with no specific temporal anchor (those are `Concept`).
- `Standard` — formal specifications, protocols, file formats, or conventions with a defined authority or canonical reference (`FASTA`, `BIDS`, `HTTP/2`, `ONNX`, `OpenAPI`, `FAIR principles`). Not for informal conventions, rules of thumb, or de facto practices (those are `Concept`).
- `Gene/Protein` — named genes, gene products, and proteins (`BRCA1`, `p53`, `TP53`, `Spike protein`, `Cas9`, `GFP`, `hemoglobin`). **One entry covers the gene and its protein product as a single entity** — this is an explicit type-specific atomicity unit, even when a source draws a substantive distinction between them. Surface forms that slug differently (e.g., source writes "BRCA1 protein" → would slug to `brca1-protein`, distinct from `brca1`) go in `aliases:` so future mentions resolve to this entry. The body covers its molecular activity, what it acts on, and its biological role.
- `Organism` — named species, strains, cell lines, or other taxonomic units treated as subjects in their own right (`E. coli K-12`, `Saccharomyces cerevisiae`, `Drosophila melanogaster`, `HeLa cells`, named phage strains). Use for organisms the source studies, engineers, or characterizes — not for casual mentions ("expressed in *E. coli*" alone is not enough; the source must say something specific about that organism). The body covers what kind of organism it is, the traits that define it, and why it is studied.
- `Chemical` — specific named small molecules, drugs, reagents, ions, metabolites, polymers, and materials with a defined chemical identity (`ATP`, `glucose`, `aspirin`, `IPTG`, `EDTA`, `dopamine`, `caffeine`, `graphene`, `lithium niobate`). Use when the source treats the chemical as a subject (its function, mechanism, properties, or role in a system) — not for casual mentions like "dissolved in EDTA buffer." The body covers what it is, what it does, and the mechanism by which it does it (ATP releases energy when hydrolysed to ADP).
- `Reaction` — named chemical or biochemical reactions, named reaction classes, and named transformations (`Suzuki coupling`, `Maillard reaction`, `Diels-Alder reaction`, `oxidative phosphorylation`, `PCR amplification`, `Golden Gate assembly`). Use for reactions referred to by name. Unnamed reactions described only by their reactants and products stay in `Concept` or appear inline in the relevant chemical or method entry.
- `Place` — geographic locations the source treats as subjects in their own right, not as incidental settings (e.g., `Galápagos Islands` in evolutionary biology, `Chernobyl Exclusion Zone` in radiation ecology, `Mauna Loa` in atmospheric science, `Cold Spring Harbor` as an institutional location). Use sparingly — most place mentions are settings, not subjects.
- `Work` — canonical creative or intellectual works the source treats as a subject in its own right: plays, novels, treatises, foundational scientific texts, philosophical or religious texts, films, symphonies, named artworks. Examples: `Hamlet`, `The Republic`, `On the Origin of Species`, `Mona Lisa`. **The test:** would the work appear on a syllabus, canon list, or in critical/scholarly commentary as a subject in its own right? If yes, `Work` entry. If no — the typical research paper, news article, or blog post — the work stays as a source, not an entry. The syllabus test here is the whole distinction; step 2 in `SKILL.md` adds only that works cited in passing or in a bibliography are not candidates at all. The body discusses the work's content, themes, author, historical context, and reception or influence; typical wikilinks are to the author (a `Person` entry), to related works (other `Work` entries), and to the concepts, movements, or events the work introduces or belongs to.
- `Quote` — named or canonically-attributable quotes, aphorisms, maxims, and famous statements (`Cogito ergo sum` attributed to Descartes, `To be or not to be` from Hamlet, `Veni, vidi, vici` attributed to Caesar). Use when the source treats the quote as a subject in its own right — discussing its origin, attribution, meaning, or cultural impact — not for every quoted passage that appears in a paper. The title is the canonical short form of the quote; the body covers context (who said it, when, where), original meaning, and reception or interpretation since. Quote entries typically wikilink to the speaker (a `Person` entry), to the event in which the quote was uttered (an `Event` entry, when applicable), and to the work the quote is from (a `Work` entry — when the work is canonical enough to have its own entry per the `Work` type rules; otherwise mentioned in body prose).

## Dates in the opener (`Person` and `Event`)

**Person and Event entries: dates in the opener.** For entries with `type: Person` or `type: Event`, the opening sentence includes the relevant date(s) parenthetically. A Person's lifespan and an Event's date are the temporal coordinates the reader needs to place the subject — what era a person worked in, when an event took place — and they belong in the opener for the same reason the main claim does: someone reading just the first sentence should get a usable mental map, and for people and events that map is incomplete without the dates. If the active source omits the date, add a well-established one as background under [prose principle 5(h)](writing.md#prose-principles), or cite another **durable source already in the vault** that states it and add it to `sources:` (a PDF with the stating page's anchor). Never guess or insert a placeholder, and never capture or research a page for the date during a wiki-build run. Otherwise, defer a new Person/Event candidate under *Entities deferred* with the missing date and the [missing-entry routes](../../../shared/CONVENTIONS.md#9-ownership-split-for-linking); the wiki-build route is a [named-entity request](../SKILL.md#named-entity-requests) naming the candidate, its source, and a date page processed through `clipping-clean`. If neither route supplies a merged entry's missing date, publish the otherwise reviewed merge and report the inherited gap under *Notes for the user*. `wiki-add` may instead take the date from a web page it inspects and cites by URL, or another source it finds under its research guide. **The forms below are exact, mechanically checked spellings:** use the shown qualifiers, punctuation, en dashes, and spacing; do not substitute a hyphen, the word “to,” or free-form biographical wording. **Format:**

- **Deceased person:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` with an en-dash — e.g., `(1643–1727)` for Isaac Newton.
- **Living person:** `(b. YYYY)` — e.g., `(b. 1947)`.
- **Approximate or partial dates:** `(c. YYYY)` for an approximate single-date Event; a spaced range with `c.` on each uncertain bound, such as `(c. YYYY – c. YYYY)` or `(c. YYYY – YYYY)`; `(fl. YYYY–YYYY)` ("floruit") when only a Person's period of activity is known; and `(YYYY – ?)` or `(? – YYYY)` when one bound is unknown. The known bound may itself be approximate: `(c. YYYY – ?)` or `(? – c. YYYY)`.
- **Single-date event:** `(YYYY)` for year resolution, `(YYYY-MM-DD)` when the specific date is salient — e.g., `(1945-07-16)` for the Trinity test.
- **Multi-year or ongoing event:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` for bounded ranges — e.g., `(1939–1945)` for World War II — `(annual, since YYYY)` for recurring series, `(ongoing, since YYYY)` for continuing events.
- **BCE/CE markers:** mark every BCE bound explicitly — `(c. 460 BCE – c. 370 BCE)` for Hippocrates. Unmarked years are CE by default, including years below 1000 — `(c. 970 – 1037)` for Avicenna. An explicit `CE` is also a valid disambiguator, especially in a cross-era range or beside a nearby BCE date; it is optional when the era is already clear.

**Placement.** The date parenthetical follows the bolded title (Bold Pattern 1) — the title stays bolded, the dates are plain text inside parentheses, no styling on the parenthesis or the years. Worked examples: `**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) was an English physicist who formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation.` (Person); `The **Manhattan Project** (1942–1946) was a United States research effort that developed the first nuclear weapons.` (Event). The dates sit between the bolded title and the role-verb (`was`, `is`, `met`, etc.), as is standard in encyclopedia openings. When an acronym-titled Person or Event also introduces a full-form counterpart, keep the date first and put the expansion in a second parenthetical: `**ILSVRC** (2010–2017) (ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge) was an annual competition.` If dates are genuinely contested or unknown in the standard literature, prefer the qualifier form (`c.`, `fl.`) over guessing a single value, and over omitting dates entirely.

## Typography for works, organisms and genes

**Work titles.** A `Work` entry's first self-title mention is bold and italic (`***Hamlet***`, `***Perceptrons***`), and later self-references are plain. Another work without its own entry is italicized (`*Hands-On Machine Learning*`); one with an entry is wikilinked instead.

**Scientific names.** Italicize the complete scientific taxon core of a binomial or unranked lowercase trinomial regardless of sentence position (*Escherichia coli*, `*E. coli*` abbreviated, *Canis lupus familiaris*). Strain, isolate, serovar, subtype and other suffix designators, common names and English descriptors stay plain (`*E. coli* K-12`, not `*E. coli K-12*`). In an `Organism` entry whose canonical title is such a scientific name, the first self-title mention combines italics with the title's bold (`***Mus musculus***`, `***Canis lupus familiaris***`); later mentions use italics alone. With a plain suffix, outer bold spans the complete first title while inner italics stop after the taxon (`***E. coli* K-12**`), and later mentions are `*E. coli* K-12`. An Organism titled by a common name (`**African elephant**`, `**House mouse**`) is bold-only. Capitalization and a two-word shape alone never establish the scientific-name role: the mechanical floor treats an abbreviated-genus title, a matching one-letter-genus scientific-abbreviation alias, or an immediate title-bound abbreviation as evidence, and a common-name equation establishes identity for links, not Latin typography. Taxon-shaped titles without that evidence, rank-marked names and genus-only titles need a source-aware judgment; the scripts deliberately stay silent once the visible title text matches. A scientific name inside a wikilink display label or a caption renders plain, since neither takes Markdown.

**Gene and allele symbols** are italicized only where the organism-specific nomenclature authority requires it. Preserve that authority's exact capitalization and symbol form, and name the authority in the run report. Do not infer the convention from token shape or apply one species' rule to another; the active source may establish that a token denotes a gene rather than its protein product, but incidental publisher styling does not establish a universal rule. When the identity or applicable convention is unresolved, keep the existing styling and report the question rather than guessing.
