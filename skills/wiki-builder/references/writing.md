# Writing an entry — fields, body, wikilinks

> **When to read this:** the run will write at least one entry. Read it once, before drafting the first — together with `references/flashcards-and-emphasis.md`, which carries the remaining two sections. The two are split only because one file of this length gets truncated by a single read; both are mandatory whenever an entry is written.

---

## 1. Frontmatter fields

### title

The canonical entity name in natural casing, double-quoted (`"LambdaRank"`, `"MNIST"`, `"ROC curve"`, `"Yann LeCun"`, `"Cross-validation"`). The on-disk filename is the kebab-case slug derived from this title by `shared/scripts/slugify.py`, which is authoritative for the slug pipeline. For terms with cross-domain ambiguity (e.g., `entropy`, `transformer`, `vector`), the title includes a qualifier (`Information entropy`, `Transformer architecture`, `Cloning vector`) — see [Cross-domain term disambiguation](#cross-domain-term-disambiguation) in §3.

**Acronym-vs-full-form coherence rule.** Many entities are known by both an acronym and a full expansion (BERT, DBSCAN, PCA, RoBERTa, ILSVRC, DeiT and their expansions). For each, **pick one canonical form and use it consistently across title, description-subject, and filename slug**. Once chosen, that form is the title; the slug derives from it mechanically; the description's grammatical subject is that same form; the body opens with that same form bolded. The other form goes in `aliases:` as a slugged alternate and is introduced in parentheses on the entry's first body mention whenever it's a commonly-used acronym (prose principle 5(e)–(f)).

**Selection guideline.** Use the form the literature uses most frequently and most distinctively. Short, pronounceable, deep-learning-era acronyms (BERT, MNIST, DBSCAN, LSTM, t-SNE, ReLU, RAG, LoRA, GAN, …) beat their expansions in modern ML/CS literature and serve as the canonical title — for the entities this list names, the list itself is the ruling and overrides the tiebreaker below (LSTM and GAN stay acronym-titled even though their expansions have textbook standing). Conversely, entities whose full form is a named textbook topic with substantive standalone usage take the **full form**, with the acronym in `aliases:` — `Principal component analysis` (`pca`), `Stochastic gradient descent` (`sgd`), `Markov decision process` (`mdp`), `Support vector machine` (`svm`). **Tiebreaker — when the entity has both a common acronym AND a textbook-topic full form, default to the full form.** This aligns titles with the `tags` calibration and keeps the wiki readable when scanned by title. "Default to the acronym" applies only when both forms are roughly equally common *and* the full form has no textbook-topic standing of its own (ILSVRC, DeiT): the literature trends acronym-first, and slug coherence is then mechanically clean (`deit.md`, not `data-efficient-image-transformer.md`).

**Task-name gerund coherence rule.** Pretraining tasks, training objectives, evaluation tasks, and other named procedures take the **gerund (verb-derived noun) form** rather than the bare noun — *"Masked language modeling"*, not *"Masked language model"*; likewise *"Replaced token detection"*, *"Next sentence prediction"*. The gerund signals the *task*; the bare noun signals the resulting *model or output*. The two appear interchangeably in informal text but pull apart on closer reading: "MLM" can mean either. When the entry's substance is the **procedure** — how it's done, what it produces, why it works — the title takes the gerund and the noun form goes in `aliases:`; when the substance is the **model class**, the title takes the noun form. Corpus failure shape: a `Masked language model` entry whose body opens *"**Masked language modeling** (MLM) is the main self-supervised pretraining task..."* — body and title disagree on the basic kind of thing the entry is about.

**Diagnostic for incoherence.** Three patterns the Quality Checklist catches (item 7 for the description, item 5 for the slug, item 16 for the body-bolded form): title in full form but description subject in acronym ("ILSVRC was…" under `title: "ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge"`); title in full form but slug in acronym (`"Data-Efficient Image Transformer"` → `deit.md`); description starting with one form while the body's bolded first occurrence uses the other. Each signals the canonical form wasn't chosen up front. Fix by picking one form and propagating; **never leave a title/slug or title/description mismatch**.

### type

One of the fifteen enum values listed in `SKILL.md` — no other values. The five that carry most of the classification difficulty are defined here; the remaining ten (`Device`, `Event`, `Standard`, `Gene/Protein`, `Organism`, `Chemical`, `Reaction`, `Place`, `Work`, `Quote`) are defined in `references/rare-types.md`.

- `Concept` — methods, algorithms, ideas, techniques, phenomena, **and named model architectures or specific pretrained models / research systems**. Examples: `LambdaRank`, `Photosynthesis`, `Backpropagation`, `Stochastic gradient descent`, `Random forests`, `BERT`, `ResNet`, `AlphaGo`, `AlphaFold`, `Stable Diffusion`, `CLIP`.
- `Person` — individual researchers, authors, historical figures (`Yann LeCun`, `Marie Curie`, `Rosalind Franklin`).
- `Organization` — companies, labs, institutions (`DeepMind`, `Anthropic`, `EMBL`).
- `Dataset` — named datasets and benchmarks (`ImageNet`, `MS MARCO`).
- `Software` — discrete software artifacts the user installs, deploys, runs, or interacts with through a software interface: libraries, frameworks, applications, programming languages, runtimes, operating systems, databases, IDEs, and notable deployed products. Examples: `scikit-learn`, `PyTorch`, `XGBoost`, `Jupyter`, `Obsidian`, `Python`, `ChatGPT`. The body covers the artifact's scope, design choices, and how it fits in the broader landscape — **and** its API surface where load-bearing: class, function, and method names, keyword arguments, attribute names, module paths, command syntax, and file formats are all permitted in body prose because they are part of what the artifact is. Those identifiers still never get their own entries; they live inside the relevant `Software` entry.

**Named ML model rule — the `Concept` / `Software` split.** A named model architecture (`BERT`, `ResNet`), a specific pretrained model (`GPT-3`), or a landmark research system (`AlphaGo`, `AlphaFold`) is a `Concept`, not a `Software`. The test is what the entity contributes: an algorithmic or architectural contribution that other entries cite, compare, or extend is `Concept`; a runnable artifact the user installs (`PyTorch`) or uses as a product (`ChatGPT`) is `Software`. AlphaGo is `Concept` even though it is also a program, because what other entries cite is the approach, not a runnable artifact. Both can coexist for one idea (`Gradient boosting` as `Concept`, `LightGBM` as `Software`); when genuinely torn, prefer `Concept`. Between `Software` and `Device`: software runs on a computer, hardware is touched (sequencer, microscope, GPU, lab instrument).

### aliases

*(Optional.)* A list of double-quoted alternative identifiers the source — or the entry's own opener (prose principle 5(e)) — uses for the entity — alternative spellings (`colour` ↔ `color`), abbreviations (`TPR` for "true positive rate"), synonyms (`sensitivity` for "recall"), and other surface forms whose slug differs from the canonical title's slug. **Each alias follows the same slug rule as the filename** (lowercase, hyphens for separators), since aliases function as alternative slugs: when a future entity candidate slugs to one of these aliases, the skill resolves it to this entry. E.g. the entry titled `"Recall"`, whose source also writes "Sensitivity," "True positive rate," and "TPR": `aliases: ["sensitivity", "true-positive-rate", "tpr"]`. **Aliases that slug-equal the entry's filename are redundant — omit them.** This is what makes "casing variants" (`Cas9` vs `CAS9`) a non-category: both slug to `cas9` and resolve to the file automatically. Aliases exist only to capture forms that slug *differently* from the filename. Omit the key entirely if none remain after this filter.

**An alias must name the *same* entity — not a related, contrasting, narrower, broader, or successor one.** Aliases are alternate surface forms of one identity, not pointers to neighbors. The failure mode this rule guards against: a `Model-based reinforcement learning` entry that, because the source discusses both, aliases `model-free-reinforcement-learning` — so a future search for "model-free RL" resolves to the model-based entry, despite the body itself calling them "the contrasting class."

**Mechanical body-scan negative test (apply before writing any alias).** Scan the entry's body for the relationship-phrase patterns below as applied to the candidate-alias. If any pattern matches, the candidate is **not an alias** — it is a distinct entity, and it appears in the body as a wikilink (when it has an entry) or as plain text (when it does not).

- **Variant / version phrases:** *"X is a variant of"*, *"a version of"*, *"the {numbered} variant"*, *"FlashAttention-2"*, *"DALL·E 2"*, *"v1"*, *"v2"*, *"-2"*, *"-3"*. → Variant is distinct.
- **Extension / successor phrases:** *"X extends"*, *"X builds on"*, *"X is the successor to"*, *"based on"* with substantive changes, *"a refinement of"*. → Extension is distinct.
- **Contrast / opposition phrases:** *"X contrasts with Y"*, *"the contrasting class is"*, *"X is the opposite of"*, *"X differs from Y in that"*, *"the contrasting approach"*, *"the mirror image"*, *"the sibling of"*, *"in contrast to"*. → Contrasted entity is distinct.
- **Component / part-of phrases:** *"the {four/three} cells of"*, *"the {four/three} components of"*, *"the parts of"*, *"the strategies for"*, *"inside X"*. → Component is distinct.
- **Special-case / general-case phrases:** *"a special case of"*, *"the general case of"*, *"a particular instance of"*. → Each is distinct.
- **Related-technique phrases:** *"a closely related technique"*, *"a related approach"*, *"a sibling approach"*, *"X is similar to Y but"*. → Related technique is distinct.

**Additionally, these structural signals mark the candidate as a distinct entity regardless of phrasing:** the candidate-alias has its own equation in the body; its own `##` sub-section; its own dedicated paragraph of mechanism; its own image; or its own subordinate enumeration ("**X** uses…; **Y** uses…"). Typical pairs the test catches: DALL·E vs. DALL·E 2 (version); Chain-of-thought vs. Tree-of-thoughts (extension); Model-based RL vs. Model-free RL (contrast).

**The field must be complete against the entry's own body (checklist item 17).** Every alternative name the body introduces *for the entry's own subject* belongs in `aliases:`, in slug form: the italicized synonym of an *also called X* / *known as X* / *(short for X)* construction (Italic Pattern 8, `references/flashcards-and-emphasis.md` §5), and the acronym ↔ full-form counterpart bound in the opener's parenthetical (prose principle 5(e)/(f)). A name worth telling the reader about is a name a future search will use, and an alias that exists only in prose resolves nothing — the observed corpus failure is an opener that reads `**No free lunch theorem** (NFL)…` above a YAML block with no `nfl`. Two standing exclusions, both stated elsewhere in this section: a form that slugs identically to the filename (redundant), and the bare form of a cross-domain term (never an alias). The same-entity test above still gates every candidate — *dummy attributes* in a One-hot encoding entry names the produced attributes, not the encoding, so it stays out. `lint_entry.py` flags introduced-but-missing candidates for exactly this judgment.

**Pluralization, casing, and orthographic variants are redundant aliases.** The slug algorithm normalizes case and the step-3 collision probe handles pluralization, so an alias that *slugs identically* to the canonical filename, or *would slug identically under singular/plural normalization*, adds no resolution power: for `dying-relu`, `dying-relus` is redundant. Same-surface-family forms that do slug differently (`oob-evaluation`, `out-of-bag` for `out-of-bag-evaluation`) collapse to the one the literature uses most distinctively — typically the acronym. The general rule: aliases capture forms whose slugs are *meaningfully different* from the filename's and from each other. The bare form of a cross-domain-ambiguous term is never an alias — see [Cross-domain term disambiguation](#cross-domain-term-disambiguation).

**When in doubt, prefer *not* to add the alias** and treat the candidate as a distinct entity — wikilinked when it has an entry, plain text otherwise. Aliases are recoverable later — if a candidate that was kept distinct turns out to be a true alias, it can be merged in a future pass. The reverse — disentangling two distinct concepts that were silently fused via aliases — is much harder, because by the time the fusion is noticed, downstream entries have wikilinks pointing at the conflated slug.

**Cross-entry alias collisions are silent bugs.** Two different entries cannot list the same string in their `aliases:`, because the alias resolves to whichever entry the future search happens to hit first — the other entry becomes silently unreachable from that surface form. Before adding an alias to a new or merged entry, check that no other entry in `Wiki/` already lists it. If a collision is found, **do not silently leave it** — the run report must surface the collision (`Notes for the user`) so the user can disambiguate: typically by retitling one of the entries to a qualified form and removing the colliding alias from one of the two, or by determining the two entries describe the same thing and merging them. The Quality Checklist enforces this (item 18).

### sources

A non-empty list of double-quoted wikilinks to the source(s) that contributed to this entry. Each item is the source's literal on-disk filename — typically *not* in the slug convention — **with the file extension included**. Sources are one of two types: raw PDFs (any long-form document — articles, book chapters, reports, theses) as `"[[Author_Title_Year.pdf#page=N]]"`, or markdown notes (typically web clippings) as `"[[Author_Title_Year.md]]"`. **PDF sources include a page anchor** pointing at the page where the entity is first defined or substantively introduced; Obsidian renders it as a clickable jump, so the user opens the source at the relevant location. **`N` is the physical page number within the PDF file** (1-indexed file position, what the viewer reports as "page X of Y"), **not the page number printed on the page** — for a book-chapter PDF whose chapter starts at printed page 87, the first page is `#page=1`. Example: `"[[Geron_HandsOnML_2025_01_Landscape.pdf#page=6]]"`. **Markdown sources have no pages** — `"[[Jumper_AlphaFold_2021.md]]"`, no anchor.

**The same PDF appears with different page anchors across different entries.** One source contributes to many entries, and each entity is anchored where *it* is introduced. That is correct and expected. If an entity is discussed across many pages, anchor to the introducing page; later discussion informs the prose but needs no anchor of its own.

**A PDF and its summary note are one source, written as the PDF.** `paper-summarizer` produces a note in `Articles/` for every PDF it summarises, named after that PDF's stem; that note and `Foo.pdf` are the same document under two names. The `sources:` item is `"[[Foo.pdf#page=N]]"` — the form that can carry an anchor — and **never both names in one list** (Quality Checklist item 4). Only a *clipping*, whose cleaned note has prose of its own and no PDF behind it, is referenced as `"[[Foo.md]]"`. `SKILL.md` step 1 is where the pairing is detected.

**Legacy stub marker.** A pre-existing stub entry — a placeholder an earlier version of this skill created — has `sources: ["stub"]`: the literal string `"stub"` (in quotes, no wikilink brackets) as the sole entry. **This skill no longer creates stubs**, so the marker is never written onto a new entry; it is only *read* (to recognize a legacy stub during collision resolution — see *Stubs (legacy)* in `SKILL.md`) and *replaced* (when a merge promotes the stub — see "Stub promotion" in `references/merge.md`). Outside this legacy case, every source item is a real wikilink to a file on disk.

### created / updated

- **created** — `YYYY-MM-DD` date the entry was first created, in the local date of the run (no time, no timezone). Never changes after creation.
- **updated** — `YYYY-MM-DD` date of the most recent merge, in the local date of the run. On creation, equal to `created`. On a merge **that changed something**, bumped to today's local date — *anything* changed, which makes this a strictly broader trigger than the `read:` reset below, and the two are not read off each other. **On a no-op merge it is left alone** — see *No-op merges* in `references/merge.md`. That exception is the whole point of the no-op path: a deliberate re-run over a processed source would otherwise reset `updated:` across the vault and make an unchanged wiki look freshly rewritten.

### description

A single concise sentence, **≤ 110 characters**, whose grammatical subject is the entity itself, named in the canonical title form. The form the description uses as its subject must match the form the `title` field chose — see the *Acronym-vs-full-form coherence rule* above.

**A leading article is part of the noun phrase and is correct.** `"A cache line is the unit of transfer between main memory and cache."` and `"The British East India Company was an English trading company that operated from 1600 to 1874."` are both right — the article does not make the entity any less the subject, and dropping it produces headline-ese that reads worse than the encyclopedia sentence it is imitating. Write the sentence the way a reference work would open it. What is banned is a **placeholder subject** (`"This method optimizes…"`, `"An algorithm for ranking…"`, or a bare predicate like `"Optimizes…"`) or a **different name for the entity** than the title uses. `"LambdaRank optimizes…"` is right; `"This method optimizes…"` is not.

**Tense follows the entity's current status:** present tense for things that still exist or operate (concepts, datasets, software, devices, standards, genes, organisms, chemicals, reactions, places, works, quotes, living people, extant organizations, ongoing or recurring events); past tense for things that no longer exist or operate (deceased people, defunct organizations, completed historical events). Examples: `"Marie Curie was a Polish-French physicist who discovered radium and polonium."` (deceased person); `"The Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA met in 1975 to set biosafety guidelines for the field."` (completed historical event).

Capitalized first word — except a title whose canonical form is lowercase-initial (`k-fold cross-validation`, `t-SNE`, `scikit-learn`), which keeps its own casing as the subject: entity-as-subject wins over capitalization, exactly as the compression example below models. Ends with a period. Always double-quoted; if the description contains a literal `"`, escape it as `\"`. **Plain text only** — no LaTeX (`$...$`), no equations, no markdown formatting (`*italics*`, `**bold**`, backtick code, wikilinks). Obsidian renders this field in its Properties panel, which does not interpret math or markdown markup; any formatting displays as literal characters. Unicode is fine (`α`, `≤`, `×`, `→`). If a formula would naturally fit, paraphrase it: `"Precision is the fraction of a classifier's positive predictions that are correct."` rather than `"Precision = TP / (TP + FP)."` — the equation lives in the body.

**The cap is checked before the entry is written, not after** — `SKILL.md` step 4's description gate counts every description the run has drafted in one command, and nothing is written until they all fit. **The gate binds every description, whenever it is drafted**, not just the step-4 batch: a recovered entry's in step 7's missed-entity audit, a rewritten one in a step-5 merge — each gets counted the same way before *its* file lands. Eyeballing a sentence for 110 characters does not work; the measured failure rate of doing it by eye was 46%.

**If a draft description runs over the cap, the compression order is:** drop adjectives and adverbs first; then drop parenthetical detail and clarifying clauses; then collapse redundant phrasing into more compact equivalents. E.g., `"k-fold cross-validation partitions training data into k folds and trains k times, holding out a different fold each round."` (122 chars) tightens to `"k-fold cross-validation partitions training data into k folds and trains once per held-out fold."` (96 chars). Avoid omitting load-bearing technical content to fit; cut secondary clauses, not the core claim.

**On merge, the description is rewritten if the new source enables a meaningfully better description** (more accurate, more informative, better grammatical structure) or if the existing one has a specific defect — one of:

- **Vague but well-formed** — entity-as-subject is correct but the sentence says little (e.g., `"LambdaRank is a learning-to-rank method."` — true but uninformative; a rewrite that adds the mechanism is justified).
- **Factually incorrect** — the existing description states something wrong.
- **Violates one of the rules above** — placeholder subject, over the cap, forbidden formatting. These should be fixed regardless of source.

The new source merely "phrasing it differently" is **not** sufficient grounds for a rewrite. The threshold is "meaningfully better," not "different."

### tags

*(Mandatory; always present in YAML — at least one value when the entity has a disciplinary home, blank only when none applies.)* **One or more** Obsidian tags classifying the entity by academic discipline: each value is a discipline slug from the fixed enum of `CONVENTIONS.md` §3, restated in `SKILL.md`, **`#`-prefixed and double-quoted**, in a block-form list. This is the entity's disciplinary classification, and it is **multi-valued**: an entity genuinely foundational to several disciplines carries a tag for each.

```yaml
tags:
  - "#machine-learning"
```

…or, for a genuinely cross-disciplinary entity, more than one:

```yaml
tags:
  - "#economics"
  - "#psychology"
  - "#computer-science"
```

**The quotes are required:** an unquoted `#` begins a YAML comment, so `- #machine-learning` parses as an empty item with the discipline silently lost — always write `- "#machine-learning"`. Tags are **not wikilinks** — they need no target file in `Wiki/`, so adding a discipline tag never requires creating a discipline note and never triggers stub creation.

**How many tags.** Tag **every discipline the entity is genuinely foundational to or canonically classified under — and only those.** Most entities have a single canonical home and take **one** tag; add more only when the entity is genuinely a primary topic in more than one discipline (a polymath whose work spans fields, a method co-owned by two disciplines). Multi-tagging is *not* "tag everything related" — the *uses* trap gates every tag.

**The "uses" trap — don't tag a discipline that merely applies the entity.** For each candidate tag the test is "is this entity foundational *to* this discipline?", not "does this discipline *use* this entity?". An ML algorithm has statistical math underneath, but its home is `#machine-learning`, not `#mathematics`. A biological method may see medical use, but `#medicine` is correct only if the method is canonical to medicine (most aren't). The most common over-tagging mistakes: `#mathematics` on ML methods (the math is upstream, not the home), `#computer-science` on ML methods (the computer is incidental), and `#medicine` on biology entities (medical relevance ≠ medical home). Tag the discipline that *owns* the entity — where it would be a primary topic in a textbook table of contents. **The inverse trap — over-tagging general scientific computing tools as ML:** NumPy, Pandas, Jupyter, and Matplotlib are general scientific computing libraries whose home is `#computer-science`, even though most of their use today happens to be in ML workflows.

**When to leave blank.** Because multiple tags are allowed, the old "no *single* discipline fits" reason for blank is gone: an entity spanning several disciplines is **multi-tagged**, not blank. The polymath `Herbert Simon` takes `#economics`, `#psychology`, and `#computer-science`. **Leave `tags:` blank** (key present, no value) only when *no* discipline genuinely applies — an Event or Place whose disciplinary content is incidental rather than defining (`Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA`, `Cold Spring Harbor` as a place). (A *legacy* stub in the vault carries ≥1 tag — a rule `wiki-linter` maintains; this skill no longer writes stubs.) **YAML form when blank** (full entries only): `tags:` with nothing after the colon — the schema's only key-present-value-blank form, and *not* the pattern `parents:` takes, whose empty form is the explicit `[]` (see the `parents` field).

### parents

*(Mandatory; always written by this skill as the empty list — `parents: []`.)* A separate skill populates it later with quoted wikilinks to broader-category entries, in block form; this skill never writes or modifies a value here. **On merge:** preserved as-is.

**The empty form is `[]`, never a bare `parents:`.** The vault pins the property as `multitext` in `.obsidian/types.json`, and a bare key is YAML `null`, not an empty list: Obsidian renders it as an empty *text* field, so the type the vault declares and the value on disk disagree — and the skill that later populates the field finds a scalar where it expects a list. `[]` is the only spelling that is both a valid empty list and visibly one.

### read

*(Mandatory; written `read: false` on creation.)* The user's review checkbox — `.obsidian/types.json` pins the property as `checkbox` — and **the one field in the vault whose value is the user's to set**: they write `true` when they have read the note. This skill writes `false` and nothing else, ever.

**A bare YAML boolean.** `read: false` and `read: true`, never quoted, never `yes`/`no`, never `0`/`1`. A quoted `read: "false"` is a *string*, and Obsidian's checkbox property renders a non-empty string as checked — the entry shows as read the moment it is written, permanently, and nothing in the note looks wrong.

**On merge it is reset to `false` when, and only when, the merge adds content to the body** — new prose, a new paragraph, a new image or recreated table, a rewritten explanation, or a stub promoted to a full entry. Appending to `sources:`, appending an alias, adding a Related-footer link, rewording `description:`, correcting `tags:`, and any review-pass format fix all leave it alone. That is narrower than the `updated:` bump above, and the two are independent: every reset implies a bump, but not every bump implies a reset, because `updated:` records that the entry changed while `read:` records whether the user still has reading to do. See *The `read:` reset* in `references/merge.md` for the rule in full, including the close-call tiebreak and the no-op path.

**A missing `read:` on an existing entry is reported, never quietly filled in.** Writing `false` into an entry the user had already marked read destroys exactly the state the field exists to hold, and no later pass can reconstruct it — the review pass records the gap and leaves the file alone. The reset rule is no exception: a merge into an entry that lacks the key writes nothing — report the gap (checklist item 2, `CONVENTIONS.md` §2d) and name the merge's new content in the run report, where the user can see it without this skill inventing their checkbox state.

### Quoting policy

YAML is sensitive to colons, brackets, and other special characters in unquoted strings. Apply this policy strictly:

- **Always double-quote**: `title`, `description`, and every item under `aliases`, `sources`, `tags`, and `parents` (this skill never writes a value to `parents:`, but if a future skill populates it, the items must be double-quoted to match the rest of the schema).
- **Never quote**: `type` (enum), `created`, `updated` (dates), `read` (boolean — quoting it makes it the string `"false"`, which Obsidian's checkbox renders as permanently checked).
- Double quotes (`"…"`), not single. Escape a literal `"` inside a value as `\"`.

## 2. The body

### Body structure

- **Length.** Write the **shortest** body that gives a reader a correct working understanding of the entity — the length test is *what could be cut*, not *what could be added*. Most entries are a few sentences to two short paragraphs; a genuinely multi-faceted method may need several, and that is the exception to justify (in the run report), not the default to drift toward. The reader came for the concept, not the source's teaching apparatus: motivation, build-up, applications catalogs, implementation recipes, and explanations of neighboring concepts (each belongs in the neighbor's own entry, reached by a wikilink) all go. When a draft runs long, cut the example first (principle 7), then the tangent (principle 6) — never the definition. The hard test is whether every sentence is *about this entity*. Display elements — equations, image embeds, markdown tables — sit alongside the prose blocks and don't count toward the length judgment.

- **Headings.** Flat prose is the right default; most entries are short enough. **When an entry covers several genuinely distinct sub-aspects of one entity that earn named treatment — typically a long entry where the reader benefits from a structural break — use `##` headings.** This is the *only* way to introduce named sub-sections; bolded paragraph-leads (`**Architecture.**`) are **forbidden as section headers** (§5). The entry always opens with a prose paragraph stating the main claim; headings divide what follows. **Use `##` only** — wanting `###` under `##` is a sprawl signal, and the split test should fire instead. Heading text is Sentence case (`## Algorithm and convergence`) and plain: no bold, no LaTeX, no wikilinks (entities named in a heading appear as bare words, wikilinked at their first occurrence in the prose). Headings help in a `BERT` entry (`## Architecture`, `## Pretraining`, `## Fine-tuning`); they don't help in a `Classification` entry split across `## Binary classification` / `## Multiclass classification` — those are separate entries.

- **The split test.** When writing a fresh entry, if you find yourself writing "X-type-1 is …" and later "X-type-2 is …" as if they were independent, the title is covering several distinct things and should be split from the outset. The moment binary, multiclass, and multilabel classification each get their own mechanism description, that's three entries — though a short umbrella `Classification` entry wikilinking to the three is fine. **If the subsections are genuinely facets of one thing**, splitting would fragment the subject; use `##` headings instead. The decision: subsections that would each be a coherent standalone entry → split; inherent parts of one entity → headings inside one entry; everything else → flat prose. For *existing* sprawling entries met during a merge, surface to the user via the run report rather than splitting unilaterally (see "Wiki refactoring is out of scope" under Edge cases).

- **Bullets vs prose.** Bullets are allowed when the content is genuinely list-shaped: a fixed set of named variants, a sequence of discrete components, an enumeration of properties sharing a parent. **Don't use bullets** to break up expository or argumentative content that flows naturally as prose — the most common misuse, and it makes entries feel like fragmented notes rather than wiki articles. Quick test: if connecting the items with "and," "then," or "because" makes them read as one paragraph, use prose; if they're parallel items sharing a parent category, use bullets. **Mechanical detection of the most common failure shape — sequential steps bulleted as if parallel:** scan each list for sequencing markers (*"first"*, *"then"*, *"next"*, *"subsequently"*, *"finally"*, ordinal openings) or any bullet whose content depends on the prior bullet completing. Chained bullets convert to prose. Corpus failure: a `Backpropagation` entry bulleting the five steps of the algorithm — they connect with "then" and read as one paragraph; the right form is a single prose paragraph covering forward pass, loss, backward chain rule, and weight update.

- **Markdown tables.** When the source presents data as a table and that form genuinely serves the entry (a comparison of methods across metrics, a parameter table, a small lookup), **recreate the table in Markdown**. Tables are identified while reading the source; they are not pre-extracted into `Sources/Images/` (only figures are). **Recreate, don't transcribe verbatim:** drop columns and rows that don't earn their place, rename headers to match the entry's terminology, simplify cells for clarity. **The cells that survive keep the source's numbers** — prose principle 7 ("drop the source's data values") governs worked examples in prose and explicitly does not reach a recreated table, whose values are its content; see the carve-out stated there. **Tables are never embedded as images**, and **don't introduce a table** for content the source doesn't already present tabularly — that's prose or bullets. **Table cells allow LaTeX and markdown formatting** — `$O(n \log n)$`, `**bold**`, `*italic*`, backtick `` `code` `` (wikilinks are not — see §3). **Placement** follows the image rule: inline next to the prose it illustrates, one table per motivating paragraph, with an italic caption on the line immediately below.

- **Images go next to motivating prose.** Images from the source are embedded inline in the body by default — each one **immediately after the specific sentence or paragraph it illustrates**, not at the top of the entry and not parked at the end of the body detached from the prose it depicts. See the Images subsection of `references/media.md` for the use-by-default selection rule and the full placement rules.

- **Inline formatting.** Backtick `` `code` `` for literal identifiers (class/function names, kwargs, module paths, command syntax) — **in `Software` entries; elsewhere `references/api-surface.md` governs: zero API identifiers, and library *names* stay unbackticked, in plain prose or as a wikilink.** A bare file extension (`.csv`) and a bracket special token (`[CLS]`) are the two backticked shapes allowed in any entry type. Bold and italic usage is governed by §5 — mechanical, audit-friendly rules, not stylistic latitude. **LaTeX inside bold spans needs explicit math-mode bold.** Markdown's `**...**` does not propagate into math mode — `**$k$-nearest neighbors**` renders the surrounding text bold but leaves the `k` non-bold. Wrap the math in `\boldsymbol{}` for italic-bold (variables, the default for letter variables) or `\mathbf{}` for upright bold (operators, constants): write `**$\boldsymbol{k}$-nearest neighbors**` so the `k` renders bold along with the rest. Applies anywhere bold and math meet.

### The Related footer

A single line above the `## Flashcards` section: `**Related:** [[slug-one|Entry One]] · [[slug-two|Entry Two]] · [[slug-three|Entry Three]]`. The wikilinks are separated by ` · ` with no trailing separator and no heading above.

**Display label = the target's canonical title,** read from the target's YAML `title:` field. Canonical titles are almost never slug-equal, so footer links are piped in practice — and **the bare form `[[slug]]` is wrong in the footer** even where §3's general rule would allow it, because slugs are lowercase and hyphenated, so a bare link renders inconsistent lowercase text among the canonical-case forms around it. Corpus failure: `[[kras|KRAS]] · [[ras|RAS]] · [[immunotherapy]] · [[targeted-therapy|Targeted therapy]] · [[daraxonrasib]]` renders as "KRAS · RAS · immunotherapy · Targeted therapy · daraxonrasib" — the two bare entries break the casing rhythm. Pipe both. There is no slug-equal exception: an all-lowercase canonical title (`arxiv`) is still written piped, `[[arxiv|arxiv]]` — CONVENTIONS §6's footer rule is *every* link, always piped, and a spelled-out pipe keeps the footer mechanically checkable.

**No minimum count** — include whatever natural neighbors the entity has: a leaf concept with two close neighbors gets two, a hub concept might get several. **What goes in:** the most important cross-references for navigation — typically the body's most important wikilinks, optionally extended with one or two entities the body doesn't mention but a reader would plausibly want next (a sibling concept, a canonical predecessor or successor, a parent category not wikilinked inline). Not every body wikilink belongs here; pick the ones a curious reader would actually jump to, and don't pad to hit a number — a short, well-curated footer beats a long, diluted one. On merge the footer is additive: existing wikilinks are preserved, new ones added. **Soft upper bound:** ~8 on a fresh entry; up to ~12 is fine through merges; past ~12, surface in the run report rather than pruning unilaterally (see Merge Logic item 7).

An entity wikilinked once in body prose can — and often does — also appear in the footer; the two slots are independent.

### Prose principles

The body is **fresh prose written from scratch in the model's own words**, not extracted-and-trimmed source sentences. Follow the gold-standard summary-writing principles below — they are the same principles that make academic abstracts and encyclopedia entries read well. These govern the **body**'s prose; flashcards follow their own principles (§4).

**Operating principle (the test for every sentence).** An entry is read by someone who needs fast, accurate orientation to the entity — either encountering it for the first time and wanting the canonical description, or refreshing existing knowledge and scanning for a specific fact. For every sentence, ask: **"would a reader looking up this entity lose track without this?"** If yes, include it. If no, leave it out. The principles below are worked applications of this one test — when in doubt about a specific sentence, apply the test directly.

**1. Main claim first.** The opening sentence names what the entity *is* — the core definition or central claim. Not biographical preamble ("LambdaRank was introduced by Burges in 2005..."), not motivation ("Learning to rank is an important problem..."), not buildup ("Before discussing the algorithm, recall that..."). The thesis itself, stated directly: "LambdaRank is a learning-to-rank method that optimizes ranking metrics by scaling pairwise gradients by the change in NDCG from swapping two items." It is also the entry's stand-alone hook — a reader who has never seen the source still gets the gist. If a reader read only the opening sentence of every entry in the wiki, they would have a usable mental map. **Self-check before saving:** read just the first sentence and ask whether it's the main claim or merely a lead-in. If it's a lead-in, demote or cut it and promote the actual claim.

  **Person and Event entries: dates in the opener.** For `type: Person` or `type: Event`, the opening sentence includes the relevant date(s) parenthetically, **even when the source doesn't supply them**. A Person's lifespan and an Event's date are the temporal coordinates the reader needs to place the subject, and they belong in the opener for the same reason the main claim does. This is the **one type-specific carve-out** from the expectation that body content stays grounded in the source, and it is **for dates only** — nationality, role, what the person did, what the event accomplished still come from the source. **Format:**

  - **Deceased person:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` with an en-dash — e.g., `(1643–1727)` for Isaac Newton.
  - **Living person:** `(b. YYYY)` — e.g., `(b. 1947)`.
  - **Approximate or partial dates:** `(c. YYYY – c. YYYY)` with the "circa" qualifier for uncertain bounds, `(fl. YYYY–YYYY)` ("floruit") when only a period of activity is known, `(YYYY – ?)` or `(? – YYYY)` when one bound is unknown and the other is reliable.
  - **Single-date event:** `(YYYY)` for year resolution, `(YYYY-MM-DD)` when the specific date is salient — e.g., `(1945-07-16)` for the Trinity test.
  - **Multi-year or ongoing event:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` for bounded ranges — e.g., `(1939–1945)` for World War II — `(annual, since YYYY)` for recurring series, `(ongoing, since YYYY)` for continuing events.
  - **BCE/CE markers:** add the era abbreviation for any pre-1000 CE date and for all BCE dates — `(c. 460 BCE – c. 370 BCE)` for Hippocrates, `(c. 970 – 1037)` for Avicenna (the unmarked CE range is the default; era abbreviation is added only where ambiguity could creep in).

  **Placement.** The date parenthetical follows the bolded title — the title stays bolded, the dates are plain text inside parentheses, no styling on the parenthesis or the years. Worked examples: `**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) was an English physicist who formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation.` (Person); `The **Manhattan Project** (1942–1946) was a United States research effort that developed the first nuclear weapons.` (Event). The dates sit between the bolded title and the role-verb (`was`, `is`, `met`, etc.), as is standard in encyclopedia openings. If dates are genuinely contested or unknown in the standard literature, prefer the qualifier form (`c.`, `fl.`) over guessing a single value, and over omitting dates entirely.

**2. Paraphrase, don't extract.** Write fresh prose, not lightly-edited source sentences with their connective tissue stripped. An entry that lifts source sentences verbatim and just deletes the surrounding words is not a wiki entry — it is a redaction. The exception is technical content that *must* be precise: equation statements, formal definitions, named identifiers (function/class/dataset names that are literally those names in the source). Everything else is rewritten.

**3. Preserve technical precision.** Every claim is technically accurate. Numbers are exact, conditional statements keep their conditions, definitions are not softened. Paraphrasing must not introduce ambiguity or weaken claims. If a source says "X is sample-efficient *when* the discriminator is larger than the generator," the entry does not drop the conditional.

   **Check the scope of new claims before making them canonical.** A textbook's worked case or informal explanation is not automatically a universal property of the entry's subject. Check words such as *always*, *only*, *guaranteed*, and *requires* against the surrounding assumptions, definitions, and equations. If a new claim remains inconsistent or unsupported, retain only what the source establishes; otherwise defer that clause and report the conflict with its page. Do not copy a suspect guarantee into the description or flashcard, or silently replace it with background knowledge. For a conflict with existing entry content, follow `references/merge.md`'s conflict-handling rule. Source fidelity does not settle factual accuracy.

**4. Coherent prose, not a list of facts.** The body reads as a unified passage with transitions between ideas, not a stack of disconnected assertions. Bullets are reserved for the genuinely list-shaped content described under *Bullets vs prose* above; expository or argumentative content is prose.

**5. Self-contained.** A reader who has never seen any of the sources should still be able to read the entry on its own. Define terms on first use; don't write "as we'll see in [[other entry]]" or "recall from the source." Source-meta phrasings ("the chapter explains...", "this paper argues...") never appear: an entry describes its subject, not the documents that happen to describe its subject. This principle has seven sub-rules — (a) through (g) — covering the specific failure shapes and the one canonical positive pattern (acronym introduction). Cross-references to "prose principle 5" elsewhere refer to whichever sub-rule fits the citing context.

  **(a) The source-meta phrasing blacklist.** Any of these in the body is a violation: *the paper*, *the chapter*, *the book*, *the article*, *the author*, *the authors*, *the source*, *this source*, *as mentioned/discussed/noted/described/shown above/below/earlier/previously/later*, *in the previous section*, *as we saw*, *the figure above/below*. The shape of the fix is always the same: replace the source-meta noun with either a named person, a named work the wiki treats as an entity, or the entry's own subject acting as the grammatical subject.

  **One narrow exception: the phrase names a specific work the wiki treats as an entity.** *"The authors of SGDR recommend..."* and *"the GPT-3 paper"* are both fine, because the attribution lands on a named thing a reader can look up. Bare *"the authors"* and bare *"the paper"* are not, because they point at a document the reader of this entry cannot see. The test is whether removing the source from the reader's hands breaks the sentence.

  **(b) Biographical-attribution drift into source-meta.** The most insidious failure shape: biographical attribution that starts grammatically clean and drifts into "the authors" as a continuation — *"introduced in a 2018 paper by Gupta et al., the authors propose..."*, where the year and the names invite the drift. Fix by rephrasing around the entity itself: ❌ *"the authors used ResNet and ViT for vision and a GPT-2-like model for text"* → ✓ *"CLIP uses ResNet and ViT for vision and a GPT-2-like model for text"*. ❌ *"the authors recommend starting with a short round"* → ✓ *"Loshchilov and Hutter recommend…"* (name the people) or ✓ *"the authors of SGDR recommend…"* (name the work — fine, because *SGDR* is the entity here, not "the paper"). ❌ *"introduced in a 2018 paper by V. Gupta et al."* → ✓ *"introduced by Gupta et al. in 2018"*: the *paper* drops out, the people and the year stay.

  **(c) Author-or-book framing-attribution drift.** Attributing a *framing* or *interpretation* to a named source reads as source-meta even when no (a)-blacklisted word appears: *"In Géron's framing the loss is written as..."*, *"Prince's book groups the methods by four mechanisms..."*. Fix the same way — make the entry's own subject the grammatical subject: ✓ *"The loss can also be written directly as a reconstruction loss plus a latent loss..."*. Naming a person who *did* the work is fine ("Loshchilov and Hutter introduced the warm-restart schedule in 2017"); attributing an *exposition* is not. When two sources frame the same concept differently, pick the better framing and write it as the fact.

  **(d) Source-internal references.** Phrases like "in the OECD life-satisfaction example," "the case study," "the experiment described above," or "the dataset" use the definite article to invoke specific elements of the source as if the reader had read it — but the wiki reader hasn't. If a worked example or named scenario genuinely helps the entry, introduce it inline so it stands alone ("Predicting a country's life-satisfaction score from its GDP per capita: a $k=3$ KNN regression averages the scores of the three nearest countries…") — not via a back-reference. If it can't earn that introduction, drop it. The failure shape to watch for is "the X" where X is a source-specific noun phrase the reader has no prior context for.

  **(e) Acronyms are introduced in parentheses after the full form on first use, then used in the acronym form thereafter.** Write "the **$\boldsymbol{k}$-nearest neighbors** algorithm (KNN)..." on first mention, then "KNN" subsequently: the full form comes first, the parenthetical establishes the binding. This applies to any acronym, abbreviation, or short form the body uses after first mention — including the entry's own aliases when the body uses one rather than repeating the title. The YAML `aliases:` field does not introduce an acronym to the reader; the body prose has to do it explicitly. **The entry's own subject always shows its acronym ↔ full-form counterpart on first mention** whenever a commonly-used acronym exists — `**Machine learning** (ML)`, `**Principal component analysis** (PCA)` — whether or not the body uses the short form again, since the binding is useful identification for a reader looking the entity up. Do the same for other terms the body introduces; omit only obscure acronyms, or a short form for a term mentioned once and never reused, where the parenthetical is clutter. The parenthetical sits **outside** any bold span around the title (`**…neighbors** (KNN)`, not `**…neighbors (KNN)**`): the title is the bolded element, the binding is annotation.

  **(f) Acronym-titled entries invert the (e) pattern.** When the entry's canonical title is itself an acronym, open with `**ACRONYM** (Full Expansion) is...` so the bolded title still comes first and the expansion follows as annotation. Examples: `**DBSCAN** (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is a clustering algorithm...`; `**t-SNE** (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) is a dimensionality-reduction method...`; `**MLOps** (ML operations) is the operations practice for deploying and maintaining ML systems...`. The choice between (e) and (f) is determined by the title field — whichever form the canonical title takes is the form bolded first in the body; the other form is the parenthetical annotation.

  **(g) Contested-topic exemption.** Entries on genuinely contested topics (philosophical positions, historical interpretations, religious doctrines) may present competing views as parallel positions with light attribution like "the materialist reading argues …" — see *Contested-topic exemption* under Conflict handling for when this applies. The (a)–(d) prohibitions otherwise apply.

**6. Drop everything that doesn't serve the entry.** This includes (non-exhaustively): motivation paragraphs, recap of prior chapters, "you may wonder why...", hedging ("it might seem at first glance that..."), pedagogical worked examples (principle 7 governs the rare one that stays), restatements of the same idea in different words, historical anecdotes unless the history is itself the subject, asides about the source author's preferences, **implementation recipes and how-to detail** (name the idea in a clause; the procedure is the source's to teach, and the anchor in `sources:` is how the reader gets back to it), **explanations of neighboring concepts** (one clause and a wikilink — the explanation lives in the neighbor's entry), and phrases that exist to fill space rather than carry meaning ("it is important to note that", "in some sense", "it should be mentioned"). Cutting these is what wiki entries are *for*. The observed failure shape is an entry that balloons past its concept because it kept the source's pedagogy — a multi-paragraph spam-filter narrative with three figures inside what should be a compact definition of machine learning.

**7. Examples are rare, short, and abstract.** Include an example **only when the concept cannot be understood without one** — when the definition and mechanism alone would leave a careful reader unsure what the thing *does*. Most entries need none: the source's worked examples are teaching apparatus, not content. When one earns its place it is **at most one example, at most ~2 sentences, woven into the prose** — never a multi-paragraph walkthrough, never a second example restating the first, never a narrative with its own figures. And it illustrates rather than transcribes: when the source supplies a worked example with specific numbers, copy the *pattern* but not the source's values. **The test:** for each number or name, ask "if I replaced this with 'a specific value' / 'a particular country' / 'the data', would the reader still learn the concept?" If yes, it's over-detail — drop or abstract it. If no, it's content and stays. **What stays:** the worked-example *scenario* (introduced inline per (d) above), the values that ARE the example's point (the chosen $k$, the threshold under discussion), qualitative magnitude claims when magnitude is the point, and universal facts about the concept (`$R^2 = 1$` for perfect predictions; the 68-95-99.7 rule). **What goes:** the source's data values, exact computed results, and fitted parameter values. Instead of "the three OECD countries closest to Puerto Rico's \$33,442 are Poland (\$32,238, satisfaction 6.1), Portugal (5.4), and Estonia (5.7); averaging gives $5.73$," write "find the three OECD countries with the most similar GDPs and average their satisfaction scores." The pattern survives; the specifics are gone.

  **Recreated tables are outside this principle — they keep their values.** This principle governs a *worked example in prose*: a procedure demonstrated on numbers the source picked to demonstrate it with. It does not reach a table the source itself presents tabularly and the entry recreates in Markdown (*Body Structure → Markdown tables*, and the parenthetical in `references/media.md`). There the values are not a demonstration, they *are* the finding — a benchmark comparison, a parameter table, a small lookup — and stripping them out leaves an empty grid with a caption. A table has its own discipline instead, and it is a selection rule rather than an abstraction rule: recreate, don't transcribe — drop the columns and rows that don't earn their place, rename headers to the entry's terminology, simplify cells. What the carve-out does **not** license is tabulating a worked example's intermediate steps: that is this principle's prose case wearing a grid, and the tables rule already forbids introducing a table for content the source doesn't present tabularly. Checklist item 15 draws the line in the same place.

**8. Include equations, in LaTeX — in body prose only.** When the source defines, characterizes, or quantifies a concept with an equation — **or describes such a calculation in prose without typesetting it** — the **body includes that equation as LaTeX**: paraphrasing math into words ("precision is the ratio of true positives to all positive predictions") softens load-bearing technical content, and a calculation the source states in words is *provided*, not absent — "subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation" is the standardization formula, and the entry typesets it as one. This applies to every formal definition, named quantity, metric, loss function, probability law, rate, or fraction the source states by either route. **The defining equation of the entry's subject or of a named quantity goes in a `$$...$$` display block on its own line** — Obsidian renders it centered — while inline `$...$` carries symbol references and short expressions woven into sentences, never the defining equation itself. Symbols, variables, and Greek letters in surrounding **body** prose also use LaTeX (`$\alpha$`, `$x_i$`, `$\Delta\text{NDCG}$`, `$k$-fold`), never plain text. Symbols follow the vault's notation standard, every symbol is bound in nearby prose, and a source equation in off-standard notation is **normalized, never transcribed** — the full rules (the coverage bar, display-vs-inline, the notation table, normalization) are in `references/equations.md`, read before typesetting the first equation of the run. YAML frontmatter never contains LaTeX (see `description`). Equations carry meaning prose cannot replicate; including them is non-negotiable when the source provides them — and stating the calculation in words is providing it.

  **Numbers in body prose: LaTeX for math, plain text for everyday quantities.** Use LaTeX when the number is a math object — class labels in a binary convention (`$\{-1, +1\}$`), indices and subscripted values (`$x_1$`, `$\sigma^2$`), numbers in or referencing an equation, and bounds (`$0 \le p \le 1$`). Use plain text when it's a plain quantity — counts ("5 trials"), dates ("1975"), page numbers, informally reported parameter values ("learning rate of 0.001"), everyday percentages, currency. Named-digit metrics keep the digit plain and spell the name as the field writes it (`F1 score` — the `1` sits directly after the `F` with no hyphen, and is a name fragment rather than a variable, so it is plain text, not `$F_1$`; the subscript form belongs only inside equations, and spellings like `F-measure` go in `aliases:`), while `$k$-fold` keeps the `k` in LaTeX because it is a variable. Test: if you could substitute a variable, it's math; if it's a fixed real-world quantity, it's plain. **Decorative `$...$` wrapping is wrong** — don't wrap plain numbers in math delimiters because nearby numbers happen to be in LaTeX; every `$...$` pair opens a math region and contributes to parser collisions.

  **Literal `$` characters need escaping.** When `$` appears in body prose as a currency sign or any other literal-dollar use, write `\$`. Otherwise Obsidian's parser pairs unescaped `$` characters as math delimiters — a single unescaped `$33` followed later by another unescaped `$` will mathify everything between them, garbling the rendering with italicized text, lost dollar glyphs, and dropped punctuation. Write `the OECD lists Poland at \$32,238 GDP per capita`. The same applies to regex anchors, shell prompts, and environment variables.

**9. Short sentences — one idea each.** Most sentences stay under ~25 words, and **a sentence that reaches 35 words gets split** (`lint_entry.py` flags these mechanically; checklist item 9). Two failure shapes account for most violations. First, the chained claim: a definition, its contrast, and its consequence strung through em-dashes, semicolons and parentheticals into one 50-word span — split it; the em-dash clause becomes the next sentence, the parenthetical becomes its own statement. Second, the packed enumeration: four wikilinked items, each towing an explanatory clause, inside one sentence — give each idea its own short sentence, or use the list form where *Bullets vs prose* allows it. Density is not a virtue here: a reader should never have to re-read a sentence to find its subject, and splitting is almost always free.

## 3. Wikilinks and naming

### Link form

**Wikilinks in body prose and the Related footer use the piped form `[[slug|Display Label]]` when the display label differs from the slug, and the bare form `[[slug]]` when the display label would equal the slug.** The slug is the filename without `.md`; the display label is the natural casing for the context. The piped form keeps filenames URL-safe while preserving canonical, readable text in the rendered note; the bare form drops the pipe when there's nothing to disambiguate. Obsidian renders `[[overfitting]]` and `[[overfitting|overfitting]]` identically, so when target and label match the pipe is noise. **The test:** mentally render both forms; if they look identical, drop the pipe. The pipe IS needed when the label differs from the slug — by case (`[[mnist|MNIST]]`), by spacing (`[[k-nearest-neighbors|k-nearest neighbors]]`), by alias resolution (`[[recall|TPR]]`), or any other rendering difference. (The Related footer overrides this in one direction — see §2.)

**First occurrence only in body prose.** Mentions of **other** entities that have wiki entries are wrapped inline — **first occurrence only** (subsequent mentions of the same entity in body prose stay bare text, matching standard wiki convention). **Possessive and partitive references count as mentions.** A body that says "scikit-learn transformers" or "Obama's first term" or "Python's `dict`" is mentioning the entity — wikilink the first such occurrence: `[[scikit-learn]] transformers`, `[[barack-obama|Obama]]'s first term`, `[[python|Python]]'s \`dict\``. The rule's intuition is *any naming of the entity*, not just "the entity as a standalone noun phrase." Entities mentioned inside LaTeX math (e.g., `$\Delta\text{NDCG}$`) can't be wikilinked from inside the math, so the first **prose** occurrence outside math gets the wikilink.

**Wikilinks do not appear in image captions, in table captions, or in table cells** — none of those zones allow wikilink syntax. An entity worth navigation gets wikilinked in the surrounding prose that motivates the table, not in a cell.

**No self-links.** An entry never wikilinks to its own title — mentions of the entry's own subject appear as bare text (the LambdaRank entry writes "LambdaRank", not `[[lambdarank|LambdaRank]]`), since a self-link is circular and Obsidian renders it as a redundant tag pointing at the current file. **Exception:** the first appearance of the title in the body is **bolded** per Bold Pattern 1 (§5) — bolded, but still not wikilinked. Subsequent mentions are bare.

### Display-label casing

Display-label casing follows standard English:

- **Proper nouns and acronyms** keep canonical case everywhere: `[[mnist|MNIST]]`, `[[lambdarank|LambdaRank]]`, `[[catboost|CatBoost]]`, `[[yann-lecun|Yann LeCun]]`.
- **Common nouns mid-sentence** are lowercase: `...counts read off the [[confusion-matrix|confusion matrix]]...`, `...tuning the [[decision-threshold|decision threshold]]...`.
- **Common nouns at the start of a sentence** are Sentence case: `[[cross-validation|Cross-validation]] estimates how well...`.
- **In the Related footer**, Sentence case for common nouns and canonical case for proper nouns and acronyms: `**Related:** [[precision|Precision]] · [[recall|Recall]] · [[roc-curve|ROC curve]] · [[mnist|MNIST]]`.
- **When the source uses an alias form, preserve that form in the display label.** A source saying "TPR" becomes `[[recall|TPR]]`, not `[[recall|recall]]`; a source saying "Lambda Rank" becomes `[[lambdarank|Lambda Rank]]`, not `[[lambdarank|LambdaRank]]`. The slug points to the canonical entry; the rendered text stays faithful to the source's vocabulary while still consolidating to a single entry.
- **Display labels are plain text — no LaTeX, no markdown formatting.** Obsidian renders the text after the pipe as plain text in link styling; it does not interpret `$...$` math, `**bold**`, `*italic*`, or backtick code inside display labels. When an entity's canonical name contains a mathematical variable (e.g., "$k$-nearest neighbors", "$\chi^2$ test"), the display label uses the plain form (`k-nearest neighbors`, `chi-squared test`); if the variable matters typographically, set it in LaTeX in the surrounding prose, separate from the wikilink. Example: write "the [[k-nearest-neighbors|k-nearest neighbors]] algorithm uses $k$ training examples" rather than "the [[k-nearest-neighbors|$k$-nearest neighbors]] algorithm." Unicode characters render fine in display labels (`α`, `Δ`, `χ²`) — those are just text, not markup.

### What earns a wikilink

**The wikilinking decision uses the same substance bar as entry creation.** A mention is worth wikilinking only if the entity itself is worth being a navigable node — would the reader genuinely benefit from jumping to an entry for this thing? Passing mentions, glancing references, and bare name-drops do not qualify, even when the slug happens to exist. Wikilink only what deserves to be looked up; leave the rest as bare text.

**Positive trigger — when a body paragraph attributes mechanism, architecture, or method to a named entity, that entity has cleared the substance bar and should be a step-2 candidate.** Phrase signals: *"X's architecture is…"*, *"X works by…"*, *"X introduced [mechanism]"*, *"X uses [technique]"*, *"X's objective is…"*, *"X solves [problem] by…"* — followed by mechanism-level detail (more than one sentence of *how* the entity works, not just *that* it exists). Create the full entry from that coverage and wikilink it. If the coverage genuinely cannot support a full entry, the mention stays plain text and the entity is reported under *Entities deferred* — never a placeholder file. Corpus failure shape — substantive name-drop without a node: a `BLIP-2` entry giving a multi-sentence treatment of *BLIP itself* (its MED encoder-decoder architecture, ITM/ITC/LM objectives, CapFilt mechanism) with no `blip.md` entry. The trigger fired; the entry should have been created.

**A bare list of names is not a trigger, however major the names are.** A `Gradient boosting` entry ending "popular implementations include XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM" teaches the reader nothing about any of the three, so all three stay bare text. Naming a thing is not teaching about it, and only the second earns a node. What flips such a list into trigger territory is the source going on to say *how* one of them differs: "CatBoost handles categorical features with ordered target statistics rather than one-hot encoding" is mechanism, and that name earns a node. The rule generalizes: if the body text would *teach the reader about* the named entity rather than merely acknowledge it, the entity deserves a navigable node, and step 2 (or the missed-entity audit) creates its entry.

**Every wikilink target in body prose and the Related footer must be a real file in `Wiki/`** — an entry that already existed, or one this run writes. An entity worth linking that has no entry either becomes a full entry (the positive trigger above) or stays bare text; **no placeholder files, ever**. Frontmatter wikilinks in `parents:` follow their own field definition. Update or create the `**Related:**` footer per §2; the footer names only targets that exist.

### Cross-domain term disambiguation

Many terms have well-known, substantively different meanings across disciplines: `entropy` → `Information entropy` / `Thermodynamic entropy`; `transformer` → `Transformer architecture` / `Electrical transformer`; `vector` → `Mathematical vector` / `Cloning vector` / `Attack vector` / `Disease vector`; `domain` → `Function domain` / `Protein domain` / `Internet domain`; `bias` → `Statistical bias` / `Cognitive bias` / `Voltage bias`; `kernel` → `OS kernel` / `Kernel method` / `Convolution kernel`. Also `gradient`, `field`, `function`, `activation`, and the common nouns in test (c). **The list is illustrative, not exhaustive** — the same qualifying rule applies to *any* term that meets the recognition tests.

**How to recognize a cross-domain-ambiguous term.** Apply these tests *before* writing the title for any candidate that is a single common word or a short common-word noun phrase:

- **(a) Dictionary / Wikipedia test.** Search the term as a Wikipedia article. If the canonical landing is a *disambiguation page* — "Tensor (disambiguation)", "Policy (disambiguation)" — the term is cross-domain ambiguous. (Wikipedia disambiguation is the broadest available proxy for "this word means different things in different fields.") The bias is to **qualify even when in doubt**: a borderline candidate that turns out to be unambiguous costs nothing to qualify, but an unqualified bare slug that turns out to be ambiguous causes silent cross-domain collisions later.
- **(b) Drafting test.** If you find yourself wanting to write the description as "In machine learning, X is …" / "In biology, X is …" — that "in $DISCIPLINE" hedge is the skill *sensing* the ambiguity. Strip the hedge and qualify the title instead: `Tensor` → `Machine learning tensor`, `Policy` → `Reinforcement learning policy`, `Return` → `Reinforcement learning return`. The description then reads unhedged: "A reinforcement learning return is …"
- **(c) Common-noun test.** Single common English nouns (`model`, `policy`, `label`, `function`, `domain`, `field`, `kernel`, `vector`, `bias`, `gradient`, `tensor`, `return`, `entropy`, `attention`, `transformer`, `inertia`, `regression`, `classification`, `clustering`, `shrinkage`, `temperature`, `normalization`, `ensemble`, `cell`, `agent`) are almost always polysemous across disciplines and are qualified by default. The exception is rare — a common noun whose discipline-specific sense has so eclipsed the others that no qualifier is needed (`Logistic regression` is unambiguous as-is, because the phrase doesn't appear in other disciplines under that name).
- **(d) Compound-noun escape hatch.** Two-or-more-word compounds that already include a discipline-specific qualifier (`Linear regression`, `Decision tree`, `Random forest`, `Neural network`) are typically NOT ambiguous — the compound disambiguates itself. Apply (a)–(c) to the head noun, not the modifier: `regression` alone is flagged; `linear regression` is not.

**The rule: qualify proactively on first creation.** When (a), (b), or (c) fires, the title must be qualified — never claim the bare slug for a single sense, even if the current source uses the bare term throughout. The disambiguating qualifier is part of the title. **Prefer the most natural English noun- or adjective-form compound** (`Thermodynamic entropy`, `Transformer architecture`) over a terse parenthetical tag like `Transformer (ML)`; a descriptive compound reads better in running prose and as a slug. **The compound must *add a qualifier to the candidate term*, not *replace it with a different name*** — `Thermodynamic entropy` adds `thermodynamic` to `entropy`, and the candidate word stays in the title. **A recognized alternative name for the same concept — the kind of surface form you would otherwise record as an *alias* — is not a qualifier-compound, so don't promote it to the title to dodge the parenthetical** (`Cluster analysis` for `clustering`, `Categorization` for `classification`); the alternative name goes in `aliases:`. **Parenthetical disambiguation is the form for terms with no graceful compound:** common nouns like `feature`, `label`, `filter`, and `clustering` have none (`Machine learning feature` is awkward and arguably wrong), so a *descriptive* parenthetical is correct — `Feature (machine learning)`, `Filter (convolution kernel)`, `Clustering (machine learning)` (with `cluster-analysis` as an alias). The parenthetical names the sense; it is never a terse abbreviation like `(ML)`. **The bare slug is reserved and unused** — never `entropy.md`, `transformer.md`, `vector.md`, `feature.md`.

**Base-term rule for parenthetical titles.** When a title carries a parenthetical disambiguator, the **base term** — the title with the parenthetical removed (`Feature` for `Feature (machine learning)`) — is what gets used by: the body's bolded opener, the description's grammatical subject, the item-7 / item-16 title-coherence checks, and the flashcard answer (line 3, along with its line-1 leak check). The parenthetical disambiguates the filename and the entry's identity; it does not appear as the running-prose subject. The slug still derives from the *full* title (`Feature (machine learning)` → `feature-machine-learning`: the main pipeline maps the parentheses to `-` and collapses the run), so the filename is unaffected. Compound qualifiers like `Information entropy` are *not* affected — there the opener bolds the full compound, which already equals the title.

**Canonical name is the contextually-resolved sense, not the source's literal wording.** When a source uses a bare ambiguous term, decide which sense it means from context and record the *qualified* form as the candidate's canonical name: a deep-learning paper writing "the entropy of the predictive distribution" produces a candidate named `Information entropy`. Resolution and creation then proceed against the qualified slug, and the body wikilink uses that slug with the source's wording as the display label — `[[information-entropy|entropy]]` in an ML context, `[[thermodynamic-entropy|entropy]]` in a thermodynamics one. **The bare term is not added as an alias to any entry**, since that would silently capture every other discipline's use of the word. Corpus failure: an entry titled `Transformer architecture` aliasing `transformer`, or `Filter (convolution kernel)` aliasing `kernel` and `filter` — each claims the bare term across disciplines and must be removed. Distinct concepts sharing linguistic roots (`Cross-entropy` vs `Information entropy`) get their own entries and ordinary wikilinks.

**Wikilink between connected senses where the connection is substantive.** Information entropy and thermodynamic entropy share mathematical roots in Boltzmann's statistical mechanics; an entry on `Information entropy` discussing that lineage should wikilink to `Thermodynamic entropy`. A math vector and a virology vector share only a word — entries don't cross-link.
