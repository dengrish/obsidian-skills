# Rare entity types — the ten uncommon `type:` values, and the date parenthetical

> **When to read this:** Read this when the candidate's `type:` — fixed in step 2, before any body prose exists — is **not** one of `Concept`, `Person`, `Organization`, `Dataset`, `Software`. Those five cover almost every run; the ten values below are the rest. Also read the second half whenever `type:` is `Person` or `Event`, whatever else is true of the run: those two types carry a mandatory date parenthetical in the opener. Both conditions are a lookup against the candidate's `type` field, not a judgement about the prose.

---

## The ten rare types

These are the `type:` enum values beyond the five common ones. The enum is closed — no other values.

- `Device` — discrete physical instruments, hardware platforms, and pieces of equipment treated as subjects in their own right. Examples: `PacBio Revio`, `Element AVITI`, `Illumina NovaSeq`, `NVIDIA A100`, `Apple M2`, microscopes, spectrometers, sequencing machines, lab instruments, scientific apparatus. The body covers the device's capabilities, design choices, throughput/specs where relevant, and how it fits in the broader landscape — the same shape as a `Software` entry minus the API surface, since physical instruments don't have one in the software sense.
- `Event` — specific recurring or one-time events with a name and a date. Covers both **research events** — conferences (`NeurIPS`, `ICML`), workshops, competitions (`ILSVRC` — the acronym is the canonical title; `references/writing.md` §1's acronym rule decides it), specific named experiments, scientific episodes (`Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA`, `AI winter`) — and **historical events of any kind** — wars (`World War II`, `Vietnam War`), revolutions (`French Revolution`, `Industrial Revolution`), pandemics (`Spanish Flu pandemic`, `COVID-19 pandemic`), discoveries (`Discovery of penicillin`), specific named historical episodes (`Fall of the Berlin Wall`, `Apollo 11 mission`). The defining test: a specific named happening (or named period) that occurred at an identifiable point or span in time. Not for general phenomena or ongoing trends with no specific temporal anchor (those are `Concept`).
- `Standard` — formal specifications, protocols, file formats, or conventions with a defined authority or canonical reference (`FASTA`, `BIDS`, `HTTP/2`, `ONNX`, `OpenAPI`, `FAIR principles`). Not for informal conventions, rules of thumb, or de facto practices (those are `Concept`).
- `Gene/Protein` — named genes, gene products, and proteins (`BRCA1`, `p53`, `TP53`, `Spike protein`, `Cas9`, `GFP`, `hemoglobin`). **One entry covers the gene and its protein product as a single entity** — this is an explicit type-specific atomicity unit, even when a source draws a substantive distinction between them. Surface forms that slug differently (e.g., source writes "BRCA1 protein" → would slug to `brca1-protein`, distinct from `brca1`) go in `aliases:` so future mentions resolve to this entry.
- `Organism` — named species, strains, cell lines, or other taxonomic units treated as subjects in their own right (`E. coli K-12`, `Saccharomyces cerevisiae`, `Drosophila melanogaster`, `HeLa cells`, named phage strains). Use for organisms the source studies, engineers, or characterizes — not for casual mentions ("expressed in *E. coli*" alone is not enough; the source must say something specific about that organism).
- `Chemical` — specific named small molecules, drugs, reagents, ions, metabolites, polymers, and materials with a defined chemical identity (`ATP`, `glucose`, `aspirin`, `IPTG`, `EDTA`, `dopamine`, `caffeine`, `graphene`, `lithium niobate`). Use when the source treats the chemical as a subject (its function, mechanism, properties, or role in a system) — not for casual mentions like "dissolved in EDTA buffer."
- `Reaction` — named chemical or biochemical reactions, named reaction classes, and named transformations (`Suzuki coupling`, `Maillard reaction`, `Diels-Alder reaction`, `oxidative phosphorylation`, `PCR amplification`, `Golden Gate assembly`). Use for reactions referred to by name. Unnamed reactions described only by their reactants and products stay in `Concept` or appear inline in the relevant chemical or method entry.
- `Place` — geographic locations the source treats as subjects in their own right, not as incidental settings (e.g., `Galápagos Islands` in evolutionary biology, `Chernobyl Exclusion Zone` in radiation ecology, `Mauna Loa` in atmospheric science, `Cold Spring Harbor` as an institutional location). Use sparingly — most place mentions are settings, not subjects.
- `Work` — canonical creative or intellectual works the source treats as a subject in its own right: plays, novels, treatises, foundational scientific texts, philosophical or religious texts, films, symphonies, named artworks. Examples: `Hamlet`, `The Republic`, `On the Origin of Species`, `The Iliad`, `Principia Mathematica`, `Mona Lisa`, `Beethoven's Ninth Symphony`. **The test:** would the work appear on a syllabus, canon list, or in critical/scholarly commentary as a subject in its own right? If yes, `Work` entry. If no — the typical research paper, news article, or blog post — the work stays as a source, not an entry. The syllabus test here is the whole distinction; step 2 in `SKILL.md` adds only that works cited in passing or in a bibliography are not candidates at all. The body discusses the work's content, themes, author, historical context, and reception or influence; typical wikilinks are to the author (a `Person` entry), to related works (other `Work` entries), and to the concepts, movements, or events the work introduces or belongs to.
- `Quote` — named or canonically-attributable quotes, aphorisms, maxims, and famous statements (`Cogito ergo sum` attributed to Descartes, `I have a dream` from MLK's 1963 speech, `To be or not to be` from Hamlet, `Veni, vidi, vici` attributed to Caesar, `Let them eat cake` attributed to Marie Antoinette). Use when the source treats the quote as a subject in its own right — discussing its origin, attribution, meaning, or cultural impact — not for every quoted passage that appears in a paper. The title is the canonical short form of the quote; the body covers context (who said it, when, where), original meaning, and reception or interpretation since. Quote entries typically wikilink to the speaker (a `Person` entry), to the event in which the quote was uttered (an `Event` entry, when applicable), and to the work the quote is from (a `Work` entry — when the work is canonical enough to have its own entry per the `Work` type rules; otherwise mentioned in body prose).

## Dates in the opener (`Person` and `Event`)

**Person and Event entries: dates in the opener.** For entries with `type: Person` or `type: Event`, the opening sentence includes the relevant date(s) parenthetically, **even when the source material doesn't supply them**. A Person's lifespan and an Event's date are the temporal coordinates the reader needs to place the subject — what era a person worked in, when an event took place — and they belong in the opener for the same reason the main claim does: someone reading just the first sentence should get a usable mental map, and for people and events that map is incomplete without the dates. This is the **one type-specific carve-out** from the general expectation that body content stays grounded in the source: dates may be supplied from reliable general knowledge if absent from the source. The carve-out is **for dates only** — other biographical or event details (nationality, role, what the person did, what the event accomplished) still come from the source; if the source doesn't say it, it doesn't go in the body. **The forms below are exact, mechanically checked spellings:** use the shown qualifiers, punctuation, en dashes, and spacing; do not substitute a hyphen, the word “to,” or free-form biographical wording. **Format:**

- **Deceased person:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` with an en-dash — e.g., `(1643–1727)` for Isaac Newton.
- **Living person:** `(b. YYYY)` — e.g., `(b. 1947)`.
- **Approximate or partial dates:** `(c. YYYY)` for an approximate single-date Event; a spaced range with `c.` on each uncertain bound, such as `(c. YYYY – c. YYYY)` or `(c. YYYY – YYYY)`; `(fl. YYYY–YYYY)` ("floruit") when only a Person's period of activity is known; and `(YYYY – ?)` or `(? – YYYY)` when one bound is unknown. The known bound may itself be approximate: `(c. YYYY – ?)` or `(? – c. YYYY)`.
- **Single-date event:** `(YYYY)` for year resolution, `(YYYY-MM-DD)` when the specific date is salient — e.g., `(1945-07-16)` for the Trinity test.
- **Multi-year or ongoing event:** `(YYYY–YYYY)` for bounded ranges — e.g., `(1939–1945)` for World War II — `(annual, since YYYY)` for recurring series, `(ongoing, since YYYY)` for continuing events.
- **BCE/CE markers:** mark every BCE bound explicitly — `(c. 460 BCE – c. 370 BCE)` for Hippocrates. Unmarked years are CE by default, including years below 1000 — `(c. 970 – 1037)` for Avicenna. An explicit `CE` is also a valid disambiguator, especially in a cross-era range or beside a nearby BCE date; it is optional when the era is already clear.

**Placement.** The date parenthetical follows the bolded title (Bold Pattern 1) — the title stays bolded, the dates are plain text inside parentheses, no styling on the parenthesis or the years. Worked examples: `**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727) was an English physicist who formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation.` (Person); `The **Manhattan Project** (1942–1946) was a United States research effort that developed the first nuclear weapons.` (Event). The dates sit between the bolded title and the role-verb (`was`, `is`, `met`, etc.), as is standard in encyclopedia openings. If dates are genuinely contested or unknown in the standard literature, prefer the qualifier form (`c.`, `fl.`) over guessing a single value, and over omitting dates entirely.
